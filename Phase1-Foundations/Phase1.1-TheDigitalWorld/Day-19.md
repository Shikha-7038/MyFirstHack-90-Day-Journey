**Social Engineering**

## 1. 🧠 What I Learned Today

Today I learned about **social engineering**, which is a type of attack where the attacker manipulates a person instead of directly attacking a computer or system.

The attacker may try to make someone:

- Reveal information
- Click a link
- Open an attachment
- Share a verification code
- Reset a password
- Transfer money
- Give access to an account or system

The main thing I understood today is that even when security technology is strong, people can still be manipulated.

---

## 2. ⚠️ Why Social Engineering Is Dangerous

Before today, I mainly thought of cyber attacks as things like malware, hacking passwords, or exploiting technical vulnerabilities.

Today I understood that an attacker can sometimes avoid all of that by convincing a person to do the action for them.

For example, instead of breaking into an account, an attacker could pretend to be an employee and convince IT support to reset the employee's password.

This made me understand that **the human being is also part of the attack surface.**

---

## 3. 🧩 The Six Psychological Triggers

I learned about six common psychological triggers that attackers can use to influence people:

1. **Authority** – pretending to be someone official, such as IT, a manager, or a bank employee.
2. **Urgency** – creating pressure so the person acts without thinking.
3. **Scarcity** – making something appear limited or available for only a short time.
4. **Reciprocity** – making someone feel that they should return a favour.
5. **Social Proof** – making the victim believe that everyone else has already done something.
6. **Liking** – building a friendly relationship so the victim is more likely to trust the attacker.

The important lesson for me was that an attack becomes more convincing when **multiple triggers are used together**.

---

## 4. 🎰 What I Learned From the MGM Resorts Attack

The main real-world example today was the **MGM Resorts attack in 2023**.

The attackers used publicly available information to identify an employee and then contacted the IT help desk while pretending to be that employee.

The help desk reset the password, which gave the attackers an entry point into MGM's systems.

The important lesson I took from this was that **verification becomes weak when it depends on information that an attacker can already find publicly.**

Someone knowing an employee's name, job title, or other public information does not prove that they are actually that employee.

---

## 5. 📚 Types of Social Engineering I Learned

- **Phishing** – social engineering through email.
- **Spear phishing** – targeted phishing aimed at a specific person.
- **Whaling** – targeting executives or senior employees.
- **Vishing** – social engineering through phone calls.
- **Smishing** – phishing through SMS.
- **Baiting** – using something tempting to make someone interact with it.
- **Pretexting** – creating a believable fake situation or story.
- **Tailgating** – gaining physical access by following an authorised person.
- **Business Email Compromise (BEC)** – impersonating or compromising business email to perform fraud, often involving money.

I understood that these are different methods or channels, but they all have the same basic goal: **manipulate a person into helping the attacker.**

---

## 6. 👤 Why Anyone Can Fall for Social Engineering

One of the most useful things I learned today is that social engineering does not necessarily depend on someone being careless or unintelligent.

Attackers can target people when they are:

- Busy
- Distracted
- Tired
- Under pressure
- Trying to be helpful
- Trying to solve a problem quickly

The better approach is not to assume that I will always recognise an attack.

Instead, I should have **verification rules** that I follow even when a request looks convincing.

## 7. 🔎 My Day 19 Reconnaissance Exercise

For the practical part of today's task, I tried to see what a stranger could learn about me from public sources.

I used Incognito/Private browsing so that my normal logged-in accounts would not influence the results.

One important discovery was that my **LinkedIn profile could be found** when I searched for my first name together with **MyFirstHack**.

Without logging in, LinkedIn showed some information such as:

- My name
- University name
- Number of followers
- Number of connections
- LinkedIn activities
- Posts I had shared
- Certifications

To see the complete profile, LinkedIn required me to sign in.

---

## 8. 📱 Instagram Finding

I also found that my Instagram account could be discovered using a search combining my name with cybersecurity/GitHub-related terms.

However, Instagram required sign-in to see more information.

This showed me that even when a profile does not openly reveal a lot of personal information, **different pieces of information can still help connect one online identity to another.**

## 9. 🔐 What I Did NOT Find

From the profiles and searches I checked, I did not find useful public information about:

This was useful because the exercise wasn't only about finding information. It was also about understanding **what information is and isn't exposed publicly.**

## 10. 🌐 My Public Digital Footprint

From today's exercise, I realised that some parts of my cybersecurity learning journey are already publicly visible.

For example:

**Name → University → Cybersecurity interest → MyFirstHack activity → LinkedIn activity/certifications**

Individually, these pieces of information may not seem very sensitive.

But when combined, they can help someone build a more believable story when trying to contact me.

That was one of my biggest practical learnings today.

---

## 11. 🎭 Building a Pretext Against Myself

The next part of the exercise was to think like a social engineer and create a realistic scenario against myself.

Instead of imagining a random phishing message, I used the information that could actually be discovered about me.

A possible scenario would involve someone pretending to be connected to my cybersecurity learning or career interests and using information visible on LinkedIn or related public activity to make the communication appear legitimate.

The attacker could then combine:

- **Authority** – presenting themselves as a recruiter, mentor, or professional contact.
- **Liking** – creating a friendly connection around my cybersecurity interests.
- **Urgency** – making the opportunity or request seem time-sensitive.

The important part of this exercise was realising that a message does not have to contain a lot of personal information to become believable.

Even a few correct details can make a fake person or opportunity appear more trustworthy.

---

## 12. ⚠️ My Weak Point From Today's Exercise

My biggest learning was that **urgency combined with a believable professional context** could make an attack more difficult to recognise immediately.

This doesn't mean that I would automatically fall for such an attack.

It means I now know what kind of situation I should deliberately slow down and verify.

---

## 13. 🛡️ My Defenses

Based on today's learning, the main defenses I want to follow are:

- Don't trust someone just because they know information about me.
- Verify unexpected contacts independently.
- Don't rush because someone creates urgency.
- Don't click links or download files before verification.
- Never share OTP or MFA codes with anyone.
- Verify recruiters, mentors, companies, and important requests through another channel.
- Use a known official contact method instead of the contact information provided by a suspicious message.

### 🛑 Stop → 🔎 Verify → ✅ Act

## 14. 🔗 Connection With Yesterday's Cyber Kill Chain

Yesterday I learned about the **Cyber Kill Chain**.

Today I understood how **social engineering can be used as part of that attack process**.

An attacker can first perform reconnaissance, collect information about a person, create a believable pretext, and then use social engineering to deliver the attack.

So today's lesson helped me connect the attack framework from Day 18 with the human side of an attack.

## 15. 💡 What I Personally Learned Today

The biggest thing I learned today is that **cybersecurity is not only about protecting computers and accounts. It is also about protecting people from manipulation.**

I also realised that my public digital footprint is more than just individual social-media profiles. Information from LinkedIn, Instagram, university details, certifications, and cybersecurity activities can be connected together.

Most importantly, I learned that I should not depend only on my ability to recognise a scam.

I need simple rules that I follow consistently, especially when someone creates **authority, urgency, or trust** around a request.

## 🎯 Key Takeaways

- 🧠 Social engineering targets people through manipulation.
- 🎭 Attackers use psychological triggers to build trust and pressure.
- 📱 Phishing, vishing, smishing, pretexting, and BEC are different forms of social engineering.
- 🔎 Public information can be combined to create believable attacks.
- 🛡️ Strong verification is more reliable than simply "being careful."
- ⏳ Urgency should be a reason to slow down, not speed up.
- 🔐 Never share OTP or MFA codes with someone else.
