# Day 89 — Prepare to Present Yourself

**Program:** MyFirstHack 90-Day Cybersecurity Journey  
**Day:** 89 of 90  
**Topic:** What hiring managers look for in entry-level cybersecurity candidates

## Learning Objectives

- Identify four signals hiring managers may look for in entry-level candidates.
- Explain why certifications and tool names alone do not demonstrate practical ability.
- Present learning and portfolio work clearly and honestly.
- Respond professionally when asked about a tool not yet used.
- Identify practical actions to improve readiness for cybersecurity opportunities.

---

## 1. The Four Hiring Signals

### 1.1 Demonstrable Foundation

Candidates should understand cybersecurity concepts and explain them in their own words. Relevant foundations include:

- **Common attacks:** phishing, malware, and social engineering.
- **Defences:** firewalls, multi-factor authentication (MFA), access controls, and monitoring.
- **Risk and vulnerabilities:** understanding threats, identifying weaknesses, and prioritising risks according to potential impact.
- **Security frameworks:** understanding how frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK help describe attacker behaviour and support defensive analysis.

Knowing a definition is not the same as understanding how a concept applies to a security scenario.

### 1.2 Demonstrable Work

A portfolio provides evidence of what a candidate has learned and practised. Examples include security audits, phishing analysis, threat modelling, network investigations, defensive plans, Linux investigations, forensics, Cyber Kill Chain analysis, and vulnerability triage.

A useful project explanation describes:

- The problem or scenario.
- The steps taken to investigate or analyse it.
- The reasoning behind decisions.
- The findings or output.
- What was learned and what could be improved.

Simulated exercises and practice labs are valid portfolio evidence when their scope is clearly and honestly described.

### 1.3 Clear Communication

Cybersecurity professionals need to explain technical information to technical and non-technical audiences. This includes explaining findings, why an approach was chosen, what the evidence supports, and what the possible impact is.

Documentation should allow another person to follow the reasoning rather than merely see a final result.

### 1.4 The Human Side

- **Curiosity:** Ask questions, investigate unfamiliar concepts, and seek evidence.
- **Integrity:** Be honest about experience, findings, mistakes, and limitations.
- **Teachability:** Accept feedback, learn from others, and work on knowledge gaps.
- **Persistence:** Continue working through challenges and improve an approach when needed.

## 2. Why Certifications and Tools Are Not the Whole Story

Certifications and tool familiarity can be valuable, but they do not automatically prove that a candidate can apply knowledge, interpret results, or explain decisions.

It is useful to distinguish between:

- **Conceptual knowledge:** Understanding what a tool does and why it is used.
- **Exploration:** Having opened a tool or tried basic functions.
- **Guided practice:** Completing an exercise by following instructions.
- **Independent application:** Choosing appropriate steps, interpreting results, and explaining the reasoning.

Candidates should describe their actual experience rather than exaggerate their proficiency.

## 3. Understanding Job Descriptions

Job descriptions can include both essential requirements and preferred skills. Candidates do not always need to meet every preferred requirement before applying.

When evaluating a role:

1. Identify the core requirements.
2. Compare them with current knowledge and demonstrated skills.
3. Note the requirements that still need development.
4. Consider whether the role is an appropriate match.
5. Apply when there is a reasonable match instead of automatically ruling oneself out over every missing tool or certification.

This does not mean ignoring essential requirements; it means interpreting them carefully and representing skills honestly.

## 4. Explain Reasoning, Not Just Results

A structured project explanation can include:

1. **Problem:** What issue or scenario was examined?
2. **Investigation:** What evidence or information was considered?
3. **Reasoning:** Why were particular steps or approaches chosen?
4. **Findings:** What did the analysis establish?
5. **Response:** What actions or defensive measures were considered?
6. **Reflection:** What was learned, and what could be improved?

For example, a Cyber Kill Chain analysis can show how a simulated attack progresses through stages and where defenders may prevent, detect, or interrupt it.

## 5. Answering Questions About Unfamiliar Tools

If an interviewer asks about a tool that has not been used, the candidate should:

- Be honest about their current level of experience.
- Explain relevant knowledge they do have.
- Distinguish theory from hands-on experience.
- Describe how they would approach learning the tool.

Knowing what Splunk does is different from having completed a Splunk investigation. Do not claim investigation experience unless it has actually been gained.

## 6. Common Career Mistakes and Practical Actions

### Mistake 1: Focusing only on certifications and tools

**Action:** Build and document relevant work, practise applying concepts, and explain the reasoning behind decisions.

### Mistake 2: Expressing vague enthusiasm without evidence

**Action:** Support interest with specific examples of learning, projects, investigations, and lessons learned.

### Mistake 3: Letting imposter syndrome stop applications

**Action:** Assess essential requirements and apply to appropriate roles when skills reasonably match. Continue developing missing skills rather than waiting until knowing everything.

### Mistake 4: Not networking

**Action:** Participate in relevant communities, share genuine learning, ask thoughtful questions, and engage meaningfully with cybersecurity learners and professionals.

Networking is about building professional relationships, not simply increasing the number of connections.

## 7. Presenting Yourself as an Entry-Level Candidate

A concise introduction should communicate:

- **Learning:** What foundational knowledge has been developed?
- **Evidence:** What work or projects demonstrate that learning?
- **Reasoning:** What was investigated, and why?
- **Growth:** What skills are still developing, and how are those gaps being addressed?

Distinguish foundational knowledge, guided practice, independent work, and professional experience. These are different forms of experience and should be described accurately.

## 8. Presenting Portfolio Work Professionally

A portfolio should make it easy for another person to understand what was done and learned.

Good practices include:

- Use descriptive titles and organise artifacts clearly.
- Explain each project's objective and scope.
- Document steps and reasoning.
- Include findings and relevant defensive recommendations.
- Identify simulated scenarios or practice labs accurately.
- Avoid sensitive information and unsupported claims.
- Keep project descriptions accurate and specific.

GitHub can organise written artifacts, while LinkedIn can help communicate progress and engage with the professional community.

## Task 3 — Walk Through a Portfolio Project

**Project:** Cyber Kill Chain Analysis  
**Scenario:** Simulated customer-data breach; not a real incident.

The analysis mapped the simulated attack through company research, a supplier-impersonation phishing attachment, exploitation of outdated software, backdoor installation and a hidden scheduled task, command-and-control activity, and attempted customer-data exfiltration.

Organising the scenario into stages helped identify opportunities to prevent, detect, or interrupt the attack. Patching outdated software could help prevent exploitation, while monitoring for persistence, suspicious communications, and unusual outbound data transfers could provide additional opportunities for detection.

The project helped practise applying a framework to a scenario instead of only memorising its stages.

# Key Takeaways

- Hiring managers may look for a strong foundation, demonstrable work, clear communication, and human qualities such as curiosity, integrity, and teachability.
- Certifications and tool familiarity can be useful, but candidates should also demonstrate how they apply knowledge and explain their reasoning.
- Job descriptions may contain essential and preferred requirements; missing every preferred skill does not automatically mean a candidate should avoid applying.
- A portfolio should show the problem, approach, reasoning, findings, and learning—not merely list completed projects.
- It is better to be honest about unfamiliar tools than to claim experience that has not been gained.
- Curiosity, integrity, openness to feedback, and persistence support continued development.
- Sharing work and engaging meaningfully with others can help build professional relationships.
- Portfolio artifacts based on simulations or practice labs should be described accurately and not presented as real incidents.
